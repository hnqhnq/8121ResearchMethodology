## Title

**A Study on the Threshold Effect of Information Disclosure in Observational Learning: A Large Language Model (LLM)-Enhanced Behavioral Simulation**

---

## Abstract

In financial markets, individuals often make decisions by observing the behavior of others. However, early noisy signals may easily trigger information cascades and inefficient herding, thereby leading to biases in collective decision-making. We construct an agent-based behavioral simulation framework for sequential decisions to systematically compare the interaction effects between four typical information disclosure mechanisms—pure action observation (PAO), optional disclosure (OD), selective disclosure (SD), and incentivized optional disclosure (IOD)—and threshold gating mechanisms. Methodologically, we introduce large language models (LLMs) to enhance the characterization of individual behavioral heterogeneity and to conduct consistency checks on institutional comparison results. The framework first replicates the core findings of existing experimental economics research on information disclosure mechanisms, thereby validating the effectiveness of the simulation design. On this basis, we impose institutional constraints on the timing of public information usage, separating thresholds that govern when public signals enter updating from those that structure how non-disclosure is interpreted. Simulation results indicate that regimes with screening or incentive structures outperform purely optional or action-only disclosure in aggregate accuracy and cascade suppression. When disclosure mechanisms exhibit relatively weak information-screening capabilities, threshold gating can stably improve collective decision accuracy and effectively suppress erroneous information cascades. In contrast, under environments with stronger screening, the marginal effects of threshold gating demonstrate significant heterogeneity, and in some cases may weaken overall decision efficiency due to delayed information utilization. Further robustness analysis shows that these institutional comparisons remain directionally consistent across different random seeds, sample sizes, and threshold calibrations; LLM-augmented agents preserve the relative ranking of disclosure regimes while expanding behavioral diversity. Overall, threshold gating is more suitable as an institutional tool for correcting inefficient information environments than as a simple complement to all disclosure mechanisms. From an institutional-matching perspective, the study provides new analytical perspectives and simulation-based evidence for understanding information governance and collective decision-making in complex decision environments.
